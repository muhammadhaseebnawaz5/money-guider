How big should your emergency fund really be

5 min read · Updated 2026

General information only, not financial advice. Your right number depends on your own income, job security and obligations.

"Save three to six months of expenses" is the advice everyone repeats, and it is a reasonable starting point, but it is a generic average, not a number calculated for you. Two people with the same salary can need very different sized funds.

Why the generic rule can be wrong for you

The three-to-six-month range assumes a fairly average risk of job loss and a fairly average time to find new income. It does not account for how stable your income actually is, how many people depend on it, or how fast you could realistically replace it.

A better way: calculate your own number

  1. Start with your essential monthly expenses, not your full spending. Rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, minimum debt payments, transportation. Leave out discretionary spending like dining out or subscriptions.
  2. Estimate your realistic time to replace income if you lost it today. A specialized, in-demand role in a strong market might take 1–2 months. A niche role in a slow industry might realistically take 6 months or more.
  3. Add a buffer for income stability. Freelancers, commission-based earners, and single-income households with dependents generally need more buffer than someone with a stable salary and a working partner.
Simple formula: essential monthly expenses × your realistic months-to-replace-income, plus one to two extra months if your income is irregular or you are the sole earner in your household.

Who typically needs more than 6 months

  • Freelancers and commission-based earners with irregular income
  • Sole earners supporting a household
  • People in industries prone to layoffs or slow hiring cycles
  • Anyone with significant fixed obligations, like a large mortgage or medical costs

Who can reasonably aim for less than 3 months

  • Dual-income households where either income alone could cover essentials
  • People in high-demand, fast-to-replace roles
  • Renters with low fixed costs and strong job security

Where to actually keep it

An emergency fund needs to be accessible within a day or two, not locked away for growth. A high-yield savings account is the usual choice: it earns some interest but stays liquid, unlike a retirement account or an investment that could lose value right when you need to withdraw it.

The exact number matters less than starting. Even one month of essential expenses saved removes the risk of going into debt for the first unexpected bill, and you can build from there.
