How credit scores actually work, and what really moves them

6 min read · Updated 2026

General information only, not financial advice. Exact scoring models differ by country and credit bureau; check your own report for specifics.

Most credit scoring models, including the widely used FICO model, boil down to five factors. They are not weighted equally, and knowing which ones carry the most weight tells you where to focus your effort first.

The five factors, roughly ranked by weight

  • Payment history (~35%). Whether you have paid on time. A single missed payment reported 30+ days late can stay on your report and affect your score for years.
  • Amounts owed / credit utilization (~30%). How much of your available credit you are using. Maxed-out cards hurt even if you pay on time.
  • Length of credit history (~15%). How long your accounts have existed. This is why closing your oldest card can quietly lower your score.
  • Credit mix (~10%). Whether you handle different types of credit (cards, loans, mortgage) responsibly.
  • New credit / hard inquiries (~10%). Applying for several new accounts in a short window signals risk to lenders.
The one number that moves fastest: credit utilization. Paying down a high balance can visibly move your score within a single billing cycle, because it is recalculated from your current balance, not your payment history.

What actually raises a score

  1. Pay every bill on time, every time. Set autopay for at least the minimum so you never miss one by accident.
  2. Keep utilization under roughly 30% of each card's limit, and under 10% if you want a strong score, not just an average one.
  3. Leave old accounts open, even ones you rarely use, unless they carry an annual fee you no longer want to pay.
  4. Only apply for new credit when you actually need it. Each hard inquiry has a small, temporary effect, but several in a short window add up.

What actually lowers a score, ranked by how much it hurts

  1. A payment 30+ days late.
  2. Maxing out or nearly maxing out a card.
  3. A collections account or charge-off.
  4. Closing your oldest credit card.
  5. Applying for several new credit accounts in a short time.

A common myth worth clearing up

Checking your own score or report does not lower it. That is a "soft inquiry" and has no effect. Only "hard inquiries," which happen when a lender checks your credit because you applied for something, have a small impact.

Scores move gradually except for utilization, which can swing quickly. If you need a fast improvement before a big application, paying down card balances a few weeks ahead of time is usually the single most effective lever.
