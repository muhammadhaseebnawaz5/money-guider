Term vs. whole life insurance: how to tell which one fits you

6 min read · Updated 2026

General information only, not financial or insurance advice. Products, pricing and terms vary by insurer and country. Speak with a licensed insurance advisor before buying a policy.

Both types of policy pay a death benefit to your beneficiaries. The difference is in how long the coverage lasts, what it costs, and whether it builds any value you can use while you are alive.

Term life insurance, in plain terms

Term insurance covers you for a fixed period, commonly 10, 20 or 30 years. If you die during that term, it pays out. If the term ends and you are still alive, the coverage simply ends, unless you renew or convert it.

  • Cost: Much cheaper per dollar of coverage, especially when you are young and healthy.
  • Cash value: None. It is pure protection, with nothing paid back if you outlive the term.
  • Best fit: Covering a specific period of financial risk, such as the years until a mortgage is paid off or children become financially independent.

Whole life insurance, in plain terms

Whole life covers you for your entire life, as long as premiums are paid, and it builds a cash value over time that you can borrow against or, in some cases, withdraw.

  • Cost: Significantly more expensive than term for the same death benefit, often several times as much.
  • Cash value: Grows slowly at first and is guaranteed by the insurer, but early cancellation usually returns little of what you paid in.
  • Best fit: Lifelong needs, such as covering estate taxes, leaving a guaranteed inheritance, or a permanent dependent who will need support indefinitely.
The rule of thumb most advisors use: if you need coverage for a specific, calculable period, term is usually more cost-effective. If you need coverage that must never expire, whole life is built for that, at a higher price.

Why the "buy term and invest the difference" argument exists

Because whole life costs several times more than term for the same coverage, a common strategy is to buy the cheaper term policy and invest the money saved separately. Over decades, that invested difference can outgrow a whole life policy's cash value, though it also carries market risk that a guaranteed whole life policy does not have.

Questions that usually settle it

  1. Is there an end date to the financial risk I am covering, like a mortgage or your kids growing up? Term usually fits.
  2. Do I need coverage that is guaranteed to still exist at 80 or 90? Whole life fits that need directly.
  3. Do I have the discipline to actually invest the money a cheaper term policy would save me, rather than spending it?

Neither policy is universally "better." The right one depends on how long you need the coverage and whether you want the insurer to guarantee a cash value, or would rather invest the difference yourself.
